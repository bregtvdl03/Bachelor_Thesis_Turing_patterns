Before we talk about weak solutions to PDEs, we need to define spaces of functions that are potential candidates for solutions. We derive this space by weakening the conditions of differentiable functions.

Consider an open and bounded domain $\Omega \subseteq \R^{m}$. Then for any continuously differentiable function $u \in C^{1}(\Omega)$ and test function $\phi \in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)$, we have

\begin{equation}
    \label{random}
    \int_{\Omega} u_{x_i} \phi = \int_{\partial \Omega} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \nu} \phi\ dS - \int_{\Omega} u \phi_{x_i} = - \int_{\Omega} u \phi_{x_i} ,
\end{equation}

\noindent
since $\phi$ is 0 on the boundary. Consequentially, for any function $u \in C^k(\Omega)$ and multi-index $\alpha \in \N^{m}$ with $|\alpha| \leq k$, we have

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:weak_deriv_expr}
    \int_{\Omega} D^{\alpha} u \phi = (-1)^{|\alpha|} \int_{\Omega} u D^{\alpha}  \phi.
\end{equation}

\noindent
Note that for this last expression to make sense, the function $D^\alpha u$ does not necessarily have to be continuous. In fact, we can completely remove the hypothesis that $u$ is differentiable, and define the \emph{weak derivative} of $u$ as a function that behaves in a similar way as \cref{eq:weak_deriv_expr} when tested against a function $\phi \in C^\infty_c(\Omega)$.

\begin{definition}
    Let $u, v \in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)$ and $\alpha \in \N^{m}$. We say that $v$ is the \emph{$\alpha$-th weak derivative} of $u$ if
    
    \begin{equation}
        \int_{\Omega} v \phi = (-1)^{|\alpha|} \int_{\Omega} u D^{\alpha} \phi
    \end{equation}

    \noindent
    for every test function $\phi \in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

We often use the same notation for weak derivatives as we do for regular derivatives. When $D^\alpha u$ is used, we should carefully think about which derivative is meant here.

This notion essentially has the same idea of regular derivatives, but the requirements on $u$ are much weaker. To be able to talk about \emph{the} $\alpha$-th weak derivative, we show that weak derivatives are unique in $L^{1}$, or unique in measure.

\begin{proposition}
    The $\alpha$-th weak derivative of $u$, if it exists, is uniquely defined up to a set of measure zero.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Suppose that $v$ and $w$ are two $\alpha$-th weak derivatives of $u$. Then for every $\phi \in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$,
    
    \begin{equation*}
        \int_{\Omega} v \phi = (-1)^{|\alpha|} \int_{\Omega} u D^{\alpha} \phi = \int_{\Omega} w \phi ,
    \end{equation*}

    \noindent
    which gives

    \begin{equation*}
        \int_{\Omega} (v - w) \phi = 0
    \end{equation*}

    \noindent
    for every $\phi \in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)$. We can now use the Fundamental Lemma of the Calculus of Variations \flcv to see that $v - w$ is 0 almost everywhere, which means that $u = v$ almost everywhere, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

We now define the \emph{Sobolev space}, which is central to the theory of weak derivatives.

\begin{definition}
    For $k \in \N$ and $p \in [1, \infty]$, the \emph{Sobolev space of order k}, denoted $W^{k, p}(\Omega)$, is the space of functions for which all the $\alpha$-th weak derivatives exist and are in $L^{p}(\Omega)$, for all $|\alpha| \leq k$:

    \begin{equation*}
        W^{k, p}(\Omega) := \{ u \in L^{p}(\Omega) \mid D^{\alpha} u \in L^{p}(\Omega),\ |\alpha| \leq k \} .
    \end{equation*}
    
    When equipped with the norm
    
    \begin{equation*}
        \norm{u}_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)} := \left( \sum_{|\alpha| \leq k} \norm{D^\alpha u}_{L^p(\Omega)}^p \right)^{1/p} ,
    \end{equation*}

    the space becomes complete, and thus forms a Banach space.
    
    Recall that $L^p(\Omega)$ is a Banach space for every $p \in [1, \infty]$, but for $p = 2$ it also forms a Hilbert space. We can say this for Sobolev spaces as well. We write $H^{k}(\Omega) := W^{k,2}(\Omega)$ and equip it with the inner product

    \begin{equation*}
        \inner{u, v}_{H^k(\Omega)} := \sum_{|\alpha| \leq k} \inner{D^\alpha u, D^\alpha v}_{L^2(\Omega)} ,
    \end{equation*}

    forming a Hilbert space. A useful subset of $H^k(\Omega)$ is $H^k_0(\Omega)$, which is defined as the closure of $C^\infty_c(\Omega)$ as a subset of $H^k(\Omega)$. Lastly, we define and $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ as the dual space of $H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)$.
\end{definition}

When $\partial \Omega$ is sufficiently smooth, we have a more intuitive way to interpret the space $H^1 (\Omega)$. In fact, we can say that $H_0^1(\Omega)$ is the subset of all functions $u \in H^1(\Omega)$ that vanish on the boundary, i.e.

\begin{equation*}
    H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) = \{ u \in H^{1}(\Omega) \mid u = 0 \text{ on } \partial \Omega \}.
\end{equation*}

\noindent
This space is often used for models with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The models we will look at mainly use Neumann boundary conditions.

Weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces are generally only used for space variables. The PDEs we will be looking at also include a derivative with respect to time. This is accounted for in a different manner, but it builds upon the last few definitions.
